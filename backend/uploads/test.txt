Introduction
Our project is titled the Caterpillar Fleet Management Tool. Often, it is difficult for managers of construction equipment to clearly estimate the upgrades they must apply to their machines. Fleet managers desire to decrease costs, decrease emissions, and increase productivity. These metrics often combat one another. There are many different jobs that require different machines, and many releases of different machines, so it can be difficult for fleet managers to optimize their equipment. Caterpillar wishes to develop a tool to estimate the upgrades necessary to optimize a fleet.

Managers of construction equipment constantly upgrade their machines. Whether the purpose is to reduce their emissions for an EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) standard, to reduce costs, or to be able to have enough machines to complete a job, there is a constant need to upgrade fleets. These upgrades include retrofitting, adding emissions filtration systems, and buying and selling machines. These upgrades can be very costly to managers, and the right choice can be difficult to pinpoint.

For fleet managers who wish to allocate their budget to improve their emissions outputs, there are many factors they must consider. Different machines have different associated EPA tier levels. A fleet manager wishing to upgrade their fleet to reduce emissions would have to first consider whether a new, more environmentally friendly machine can efficiently complete the job. The fleet manager must also decide whether this machine fits their budget. Even if the fleet manager finds the suitable machine, there are many alternatives, which include buying a DPF (Diesel Particulate Filter) to directly filter out particulate matter, or buying a new engine for one of their existing machines. When a fleet manager wishes to allocate more resources towards lessening their environmental impact, they may wish to downsize their fleet while still efficiently completing the job. This can take many hours of important decision making. This is because there are many different types of equipment, each with different abilities, and different levels of productivity. These are a few of many decisions that a fleet manager will need to make if they wish to reduce their environmental impact. 

	The burden associated with estimating the cheapest, environmentally friendly, and efficient option can be reduced through the use of a tool that is catered to the fleet manager’s scenario. For fleet managers that have a smaller budget, the tool must give recommendations that do not surpass the fleet manager’s budget limit. For fleet managers that have many machines, the tool must give recommendations that consider the limitations and capabilities of every machine in that fleet. Due to the infinitely wide range of scenarios, the tool must take into account the specifications of machines released throughout the past decade. These specifications include horsepower, fuel consumption, size, and many other factors that might influence the cost, emission levels, and productivity of a machine.

	Fleet sizes can range depending on the manager. Some fleet managers might be contractors that own a few machines, while others could be government agencies that own several fleets composed of over 50 machines. When it comes to the larger fleets, the cost savings in downsizing the fleet can be very large. In fact, according to McKinsey and Company, after an agency disposed of 3,000 assets, they estimated life cycle cost savings of around $200 million. The barrier of researching which machines to sell prevents fleet managers from taking actions that could save them large portions of their budget. A tool that guides managers in their decision making would allow them to confidently and effectively downsize their fleet. In addition to the upfront cost savings from selling machines, there are also time savings associated with the use of this tool. The average construction fleet manager has many daily tasks that often involve time constraints, meetings, scheduling, and compliance, so the extra time spent researching ways to reduce costs is simply not feasible. Not only would this tool save fleet managers money, but it would also allow them to dedicate more time to more important tasks. 

	As machines are innovated, the standard for emissions levels is increased. This increases the burden on fleet managers to ensure their fleets are compliant with the EPA’s policies. The EPA’s in-use vehicle testing program checks to ensure that a fleet’s equipment is compliant, and if non-compliant machines are discovered, the fines can be up to $45,000. There are also Onboard Diagnostic (OBD) systems, which monitor the engine performance and emissions levels of machines in real time. These systems can be monitored by the EPA to ensure that machines are compliant. With these audits, fleet managers are pressured to ensure that their machines are compliant with EPA standards, and these standards are subject to change. A tool that estimates the emissions of potential new fleets would save fleet managers much time and stress.

	The use of this tool isn’t limited to mandatory requirements in order for a fleet manager to ensure a job is completed within a budget. The tool also can provide fleet managers with a competitive advantage. A fleet manager that can accurately estimate the effects of different upgrades to their fleet will be able to complete jobs for a lower cost, meaning that these savings will be passed on to the customer, yielding a better service.

The services that this tool would provide are currently being offered by environmental specialists, emissions testing services, and regulatory compliance specialists. Fleet managers allocate a portion of their budget for these services. A tool that effectively handles these services would save the costs of hiring third-parties. It is worth noting that this is an ideal case - an automated tool is unlikely to provide services of the same caliber as a hired professional. However, the ease of use and constant availability of such a tool would reduce the need to hire professionals. 
Methods	
	When deciding the best way to deploy such a tool, our team took into consideration the nature of the general problem. The tool needed to be easily accessible and dynamic, so a web application or mobile app were the best choices of deployment. However, most of the work would be composed of developing the underlying model, as this was the main task.

The goal of the model was to recommend actions that fleet managers could take. To make the goal more concrete, our team needed to formalize the term “action.” This is because there are a wide range of actions that a fleet manager can take. For example, to reduce emissions, the fleet manager could attach a filter to their engine, they could re-engine their machine, or they could sell their machine and choose a more eco-friendly option. In order to simplify this large pool of potential actions, our team decided to formalize the concept of an action by categorizing the pool into three categories: buying a machine, selling a machine, and replacing a machine. This simplification can be used in the scenario in which the fleet manager wishes to attach a filter to their machine. In this case, the fleet manager is replacing his prior machine with the same machine, but with a filter. This makes requirements such as estimating costs easier.

We also needed to formalize the concept of a “machine.” Because there are many models of machines and machines go through upgrades over time, just a model number wouldn’t be enough to summarize the features of a machine. This meant that the inputs to the model needed to be the specifications of the machines in a fleet. These specifications include horsepower, weight, EPA tier level, and fuel consumption. While this required more from the end user, given the time constraints and the scale of the product, this simplification was the most practical solution. To make our solution feel more concrete, our team designed a mockup of the final web page (Figure 1)

Figure 1. User interface mockup

	In addition to simplifying how machines are inputted, our team also needed to narrow the scope of the project, in terms of which machines the tool needed to support. Caterpillar offers a wide range of machines, so to gather enough data to cover all machines wouldn’t be feasible. Our group decided to focus on three machines: excavators, loaders, and dozers. By limiting the scope to three machines, our group was able to complete the project as a proof of concept. Once the model was created for these three machines, it could easily be scaled to cover other machines.

The Caterpillar Inc. project specification required that the tool takes into account the value, emission levels, and productivity of each machine in the fleet. Because our model needed to optimize the recommendations in terms of cost, emissions, and productivity, we needed to build models that could estimate the value, emission levels, and productivity for a given machine. With these three models, we could then build an optimization model that determines the best types of machines for given constraints. To estimate cost, emissions, and productivity of machines, a data driven model was the most appropriate route to take. This would require three separate estimators. Our team didn’t have access to Caterpillar data, so data needed to be scraped from publicly available sites.

Our team built the three models in parallel to save time. Some members focused on collecting price data, while others collected emissions data. For the productivity model, the data was much more difficult to find, so the model was not data driven. The hurdle with price data was the change in value of machines over time. The prices were also not publically available from Caterpillar, so the best route was auction sites. This way, data could be scraped for machines of different ages, accounting for the decrease in value over time.
Cost Estimator
For the data collection for the cost estimator, our team used various auction sites that recorded model names with prices at which they were sold. These sites include Market Book, Iron Planet, and Construction Equipment Guide. The data from these sites were scraped and recorded into CSV files, containing a total of ~5000 rows. These data pertained to only excavators, loaders, and dozers, as this was the scope of our project. The data from these sites only contained model numbers and prices, not machine specification data, so our team needed to map model numbers to specifications. After discussing this with our sponsor and reading machine documentation, our team discovered that model numbers gave indications of age, weight, addons, and other specifications that impact emissions levels and productivity. This meant that we had sufficient data to estimate value, given machine specifications. 

Once our team had the price data, we needed to create a model that estimates the value of other machines. To do this, our team decided to use TPOT. This is a tool that automates part of the model creation. TPOT selects the most appropriate model for the data by comparing metrics like RMSE or R-Squared values. TPOT selects the data preprocessing pipeline, and it automates hyperparameter tuning. For selecting the pipeline, TPOT compares 10,000 different pipeline configurations, and scores each configuration using 10-fold cross validation. 10-fold cross validation is a way to score a model by splitting the data into 10 groups, using one for validation and the other nine for training. TPOT repeats this nine more times, and averages the resulting scores. Our team used TPOT because it takes care of the heavy-lifting associated with building the model. TPOT selected a combination of SGDRegressor1, RidgeCV2, and ExtraTreesRegressor3 for the value estimation. This combination resulted in a RMSE of 28% and an R-Squared value of 0.826 (Figure 2). These values come from the comparison of the predicted prices and real prices.










SGD stands for Stochastic Gradient Descent Regressor, and it is a variant of linear regression that uses gradient descent algorithm to minimize the mean squared error loss function. The coefficients of the linear regression model are updated iteratively using a small subset of the training data at each iteration.
Ridge regression is a linear regression method that adds a penalty term to the ordinary least squares (OLS) objective function. The penalty term is proportional to the squared magnitude of the coefficients, which helps to prevent overfitting by shrinking the coefficients towards zero.
ExtraTreesRegressor works by building a large number of decision trees on random subsets of the training data, and then averaging the predictions from all the trees to make the final prediction.

Emissions Estimator
To build the emissions estimator, we needed to gather emissions data on excavators, loaders, and dozers. Because fleet managers don’t often monitor the emissions levels of their machines, it was difficult to find a publicly available source of emissions data. However, the EPA has a tool called the DEQ (Diesel Emissions Quantifier). This tool estimates emissions for machines based on the specifications. There was no public API, so the best way to gather the data was to scrape it from the estimator. By inputting various “common” examples of excavators, loaders, and dozers, we could collect data that maps specifications to emissions levels (Figure 3). The inputs for the DEQ were specifications such as horsepower, weight, fuel consumption, and other specifications. These specifications were very similar to those for the value model, meaning that these two models could share user inputs. Whereas the scraping of the auction site only required downloading the HTML, the scraping of the DEQ was more involved. The DEQ requires inputs, and these inputs are specifications of machines, so the scraper needed to input hypothetical machines into the DEQ. These machines needed to represent the average machine that a user would input into our tool. To do this, we researched specifications of excavators, loaders, and dozers that were commonly bought and sold. From this research, we created ranges for all specifications that were to be inputted to the DEQ. Our scraper iterated through these ranges and inputted ~5000 example machines for each type (excavator, dozer, and loader). For each example machine, the scraper collected emissions data for NOx, CO, PM, CO2, and HP. 


Figure 3. Specific relationships between variables and emissions levels of excavators

To build the emissions estimator, we used the nearest neighbor method of estimating the emissions for a user input. The data collected from the DEQ was very dense, and this is because our team had control over the machines inputted into the DEQ. This meant that for most user inputs, there would be a similar machine in our dataset. After the user inputs their machine, the most similar machine in our dataset would be selected, and our estimator would output the emissions levels of that machine. When comparing the estimated emissions to the emissions from the DEQ, this estimator reached an average error of 5% for each emission, which was acceptable.
Productivity Estimator
The final estimator we needed was a productivity estimator. The purpose of this estimator was mainly to ensure that recommendations from our model would still be able to complete the job required of the fleet manager. For example, while a shovel would be cheaper and be more eco-friendly than an excavator, it wouldn’t be nearly as productive. For this reason, we needed a way to quantify the productivity of a construction fleet. The term “productivity” is loose, and difficult to quantify. From our value and emissions estimators, we had a rough outline of the inputs that our model would require, so to estimate productivity, we worked from those.

From our inputs, we discovered that we could use horsepower and weight as a proxy for productivity. To do this, we had to make the assumption that a machine with higher weight and horsepower would always be more productive than a machine with lower weight and lower horsepower. While this assumption is not always true, it was strong enough to roughly estimate the productivity of a machine. We confirmed this observation by plotting the weight and the horsepower of machines, and we noticed a proportional relationship (Figure 4).

Figure 4. The relationship between weight and horsepower for each machine type

With the value, emissions, and productivity estimators, our team could then build the optimization algorithm. The main task was, given an input fleet of machines, to output similar fleets that meet the constraints of the user. 
Optimization Algorithm
Our team decided to put user constraints in terms of reductions/additions to the current fleet’s emissions and productivity, given a budget. The budget was to be inputted as dollars, and the emissions and productivity were to be inputted as a percent change. Because our emissions estimator had separate outputs for each type of emission, our team decided to allow the user to define desired reductions for each type of emission (NOx, CO, PM, CO2, and HP). The reason for a percent change, rather than an explicit amount, was because the user might not be certain of their current emissions levels. By allowing the user to input a percentage reduction, the user wouldn’t need to base their inputs off of their current emissions levels.

The key insight that guided the development of the optimization algorithm was the concept that each minor upgrade costs a certain dollar amount. This might seem trivial, but with this concept in mind, our team recognized a method in which the optimization algorithm could determine the cost associated with upgrades. Firstly, the algorithm would pick a new similar fleet, modifying one machine. This new fleet could be a potential recommended upgrade. Then, with the value estimator, the algorithm would compare the value of the new fleet with the value of the old fleet. With these two values, our algorithm could estimate the cost associated with the recommended upgrade. 

The pool of potential fleets is extremely large, and each fleet has a different value, emission level, and productivity level. This means that the optimization is non-convex, as there are likely multiple local minima and maxima. The optimization is also non-linear, as our emissions and value estimators are non-linear with the machine inputs. These attributes make it difficult to create an algorithm that determines the best possible fleet for the user’s constraints. This is why our optimization algorithm would need to make educated random selections of new potential fleets, and then determine whether the new fleet was feasible given the user’s constraints. This can be characterized as a Monte Carlo4 method. This is a stochastic optimization method that involves random sampling and iteration until certain conditions are met.
In order to develop an optimization algorithm that samples random machines, we needed to write a function that could propose new fleet samples. To do this, our team developed a “machine generator.” This generator proposes a new fleet given the type of machines currently in the user’s fleet. With this new fleet, the algorithm can then check if the fleet is feasible, and then report the associated reductions/additions of value, emissions, and productivity. If this fleet isn’t within the user’s constraints, but still improves in emissions and productivity (i.e. gets closer to the user’s constraints), the algorithm generates another fleet from the previously generated one.

The generator creates new fleets using the machine data we collected for our estimators. This way, the generator wouldn’t propose fleets that are impossible to find on the market. The fleets are also similar to the fleets in the user’s input, so the output of our model would still be related to the past fleet. If the generator didn’t take into account the current machines in the user’s fleet, the estimated costs would be too large for the model to quickly find feasible upgrades.

The optimization algorithm can be visualized as a graph, where the x-axis represents emissions, and the y-axis represents productivity. A single point on the graph represents a fleet. In order to move from one point to another, there is a cost, so the associated difference in value is deducted from the user’s budget. By generating similar fleets to the one the user inputted, the optimization algorithm traverses through this graph (or sample space), until the user either runs out of budget, or until a point within the constraints of the user is located. This occurs multiple times, so that the user is presented with multiple possible options. If after 10,000 attempts the search cannot find any options for the user’s criteria, the algorithm terminates and requires the user to alter their constraints. 







Monte Carlo is a class of algorithms that use randomly computed samples to obtain numerical results. It applies to this project, because the model needs to conclude which configuration of machines might yield a better outcome. This list is unlimited, so random samples must be generated in order to estimate a better configuration.
Deployment
A requirement for our project was a functional user interface. In order to allow the interface to communicate with our optimization model, we needed an API that received user inputs and outputted the recommended fleets. Because we wanted to focus on the functionality of the model rather than the method of deployment, we decided to go with the quick and easy approach using FastAPI5. FastAPI allowed us to quickly build an API that we knew would work well for our needs. Our project design only requires the user to input data once, and there would only be one set of data returned, so the API only needed one endpoint. Once the user submits the fields on the tool, the API handles the data, runs them through the optimization model, the API parses and organizes the output in JSON format, which is then returned to the web page for the user to read.

Our team used React6 to design the user interface. To keep the design simple, we built one page for the user input and one page for the output. This served as a proof of concept for the model. Separately, our team designed an ideal user interface for the model (Figure 5), but due to time constraints and priorities, we did not implement this interface.

Lessons and Results
	Throughout this project, our team faced many challenges. Without Caterpillar data, we had to use third party sources to find data to use for our model. It was also difficult for the team to determine how to quantify certain measurements, such as productivity or other factors that might influence the emissions of equipment. However, once presented with these challenges, we were able to find creative methods to work around the challenges, rather than changing the scope of the project. This was a significant learning experience for all members of the team, because we were still able to accomplish our goals by being ingenuitive and determined. This reinforces the notion that hard work can trump all challenges. 


	Another problem we faced was the unclear scope of our project. Because we were not able to predict which parts of the project would be the most challenging, we weren’t sure how to distinguish between the achievable and the unachievable. For example, at first we wanted to support all machines that Caterpillar had to offer. After learning about all of the different types of machines, and how vastly different each machine was, we decided that we would only be able to support very few. Also, we were unsure which features of the machine we needed to account for when predicting emissions and cost. This required a large amount of research, conducted by all members of the team. Once the team determined the approximate answer to these uncertainties, the project seemed much more defined, and it was easier to manage. This taught our team a more specific lesson - one that can really only be learned through experience. At first, our team was extremely discouraged by the fact that our project was extremely open-ended and not directly clear. However, once we worked step-by-step, researching small details to help narrow the scope, we realized we could easily formulate a clear set of steps to build our tool. The lesson is that sometimes, most of the work might involve planning the project rather than actually building it. If the planning is done correctly, it might be much easier to understand the project in its entirety, and in turn this makes the project easier to complete.

	Even with our setbacks, our team was able to complete the basic requirements for this project by the due date, which was the end of calendar year 2023. The end result was a functional web application, which, given user inputs for fleet specifications and constraints, returns recommendations for fleet upgrades. With this proof of concept tool, Caterpillar can then use the underlying concepts to rebuild an in-house tool that utilizes their proprietary data and technology stack. We have handed off our project to our project sponsor, and they were satisfied with our product.
